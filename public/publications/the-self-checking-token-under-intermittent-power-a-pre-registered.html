The Self-Checking Token Under Intermittent Power: A Pre-Registered, On-Silicon Measurement of When a Frozen Decision's Committed-Ring Re-Derivation Doubles as a Free Integrity Sentinel — and When It Does Not

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 22, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.24450553

Abstract

A frozen, deterministic, class-discriminant single-token decision encoder reduces a window of sensor observations to one 8-bit token, and a companion line of work showed how it survives intermittent power: the device keeps a bounded ring of committed input windows in non-volatile memory and, after any power failure, re-derives its adapted decision as a progress-free pure function of that ring — no checkpoint, no evolving state, bit-exact across power cuts. This paper asks whether that re-derivation, built for survival, doubles as an integrity sentinel, and where its boundary lies. Under pre-registration and on the microcontrollers the method targets, the answer is a sharp law with an equally sharp limit. The committed-ring re-derivation is a free safety integrity monitor: re-deriving the decision from the ring and comparing it to a retained one-token fingerprint catches every decision-changing random corruption of the ring — bit-rot, single-event upsets, torn writes — at the cost of one comparison (0.05% of a re-derivation) and with zero decision-changing faults escaping across 12 million injected faults (95% upper bound ≈ 5×10⁻⁷), because the re-derivation is work the survival path already performs. It is not a security control: a consistency-preserving adversary who tampers with a committed window and recomputes its error-detecting code (or replays an older validly-committed ring) is blind to the self-check (detection 0.000 in the single-chip trust domain), because the re-derivation faithfully reproduces a consistent-but-wrong decision; detection returns to 1.000 only with an attested fingerprint or monotonic epoch. The reach is therefore a binary set by the trust domain — free-safe against the corruption nature inflicts, blind against the tampering an adversary inflicts — and because a random fault essentially never preserves consistency (code escape ≈ 2⁻³², measured at zero) while an adversary always does, the smooth "detection falls as consistency-preservation rises" curve is honestly two endpoints with a trust boundary between them. The property is dataset-agnostic (identical on six sensor modalities), arithmetic-independent (float = Q16.16, byte-identical clean stream), and bit-exact across two cores (a Cortex-M33 metered with a Power Profiler Kit II and a Cortex-M4F; clean and tampered decision-stream hashes identical, and matching the host and five builds). It composes with the published competence monitor and interlock at zero cross-channel interference; its benefit is accuracy under corruption, not merely an alarm (a self-check with an evict-and-recompute recovery holds classification accuracy at 0.935 under a 20% fault rate where an unchecked policy falls to 0.646); it protects exactly the readout it fingerprints (a token-only fingerprint misses 35% of corruptions that shift the soft monitor distance without flipping the token); and it is blind by design to pre-commit sensor-side corruption, which is the sensor-validity gate's job. The step up to security against a deliberate adversary is a system matter and is measured cheap: one keyed MAC over the window and a monotonic epoch closes both the tamper and the replay gap (detection 1.000), and a real keyed cryptographic MAC on the Cortex-M33 costs 0.106 µJ/commit, about 26% less than the bitwise error-detecting code, so adversarial integrity is not a compute cost — its real cost is key management in hardware. The whole result is a characterization of previously published, separately patent-pending matter; it introduces no new claimed mechanism, and the per-deployment integrity-provisioning selection is retained as trade secret.
