The Energy Case for Tier-0: A Pre-Registered, Measured Energy Frontier and System-Gating Law for a Frozen Decision Token on Microcontroller Silicon

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 17, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.24322464

Abstract

The prevailing efficiency argument for a cheap edge classifier is made in FLOPs. FLOPs are an estimate; the quantity a battery pays is energy, and on a cache-poor microcontroller energy is set as much by memory traffic and duty cycle as by operation count. This paper measures, rather than estimates, the energy of a frozen, deterministic, class-discriminant single-token decision encoder against the efficient-edge frontier, on real Nordic silicon (a Cortex-M33, nRF54L15, instrumented with a Power Profiler Kit II), under two pre-registered campaigns with frozen Go/No-Go bands. The first establishes a measured energy-per-decision frontier: the 1-byte token decides in 12.3 µJ, against 19× (depthwise-separable CNN), 29× (a MicroNets-style micro-CNN), 135× (ROCKET), and 481× (an int8 "SRAM-wall" CNN the 32-bit path cannot even fit) more energy for the raw-signal neural and classical time-series frontier — a span of roughly two and a half orders of magnitude, with the token competitive or better on accuracy. A host study across ten datasets confirms the compute-proxy version of this result (the token is Pareto-non-dominated by the neural frontier at 20–300× fewer multiply-accumulates at matched accuracy, 10 of 10) and, reported straight, its honest boundary: a trivial linear model on the same cheap features ties the token on accuracy-per-operation, so the token's defensible value over a linear baseline is not accuracy-per-FLOP but the invariant package — a deterministic ≤256-entry lookup, a one-byte output, and a built-in label-free nearest-centroid competence monitor — and the compute/energy win is specifically against the neural frontier the field reaches for. The second campaign composes the measured per-tier energies into a system-gating law. A frozen Tier-0 token in front of an expensive Tier-N stage is an energy valve: it saves total system energy only below a break-even wake-rate, and that break-even is a closed form, r* = 1 − E₀/E_N, in the measured per-tier energies. We confirm the composition on silicon: an interleaved Tier-0+Tier-N sweep shows the composed per-decision energy is linear in the wake-rate to better than 0.1 % (R² = 1.000000), so the composed system costs exactly E₀ + w·E_N with no context-switch or cache overhead, and the measured break-even matches the closed form to within 0.1 % for two on-device Tier-N magnitudes. Translating the measured energies into the cost that dominates deployed-sensor ownership — the battery-swap/maintenance interval — the token-gated architecture runs for months on a coin cell where always-on deep learning runs for hours, and an operating-mode × domain map (literature-grounded cadences for seizure EEG, arrhythmia ECG, bearing vibration, wearable activity, and continuous glucose monitoring) shows that in continuous rare-event monitoring the token-triggered architecture is the only one delivering full event coverage and multi-month battery life. We then red-team the frontier across seven axes and report the results, including the ones that temper the claim: the energy-per-decision advantage is window-length- and channel-count-dependent (≈5–29× versus the smallest CNN, 24–481× versus a real CNN, never inverting); every model's latency is bounded with under-0.2 % jitter (a worst-case frontier, not an average one); the ratios are preserved on a second core (slightly larger on a Cortex-M4F); the naive int8 baseline is already at the scalar-compiler ceiling (a fair, not a strawman, comparison); and — a correction we fold into a companion erratum — on this hardware's DC-DC buck regulator the supply energy per decision is approximately voltage-independent (≈ −8 % at 1.8 V, not the quadratic saving a linear-regulator intuition predicts). This is a characterization, on commodity hardware, of previously published methods that are the subject of filed U.S. patent applications, measured against third-party prior-art baselines; it discloses no new algorithmic subject matter and is accompanied by no new patent application. The per-deployment operating-point and architecture-selection procedure is retained as trade secret.
