The Sub-Bit Decision Token: An Information Ceiling on a One-Byte Codebook, a Near-Universal Escape via a Frequency Ladder, a Cheap Commissioning Meter, and a Bit-Exact Reduction to Practice on Commodity Silicon

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 4, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.23962248

Abstract

A frozen single-byte "decision token" — features mapped by a closed-form affine embedding to a ≤256-cell k-means codebook, read out by a per-cell class posterior — is the base instrument of a program that studies how far one deterministic, microjoule-class lookup can be pushed across sensing modalities. This paper asks the sharpest quantitative question about that instrument: how much decision-relevant information does one token actually carry, can any cleverer way of placing its cells raise that number, and when it cannot, what does raise it? Across an eleven-tier pre-registered battery on the full UCR and UEA time-series archives, with decision bands fixed before measurement and negatives kept, we establish four things. (1) A bound. The token carries, bias-corrected and shuffle-null-subtracted, only ≈0.42 bits of net label information on average (bootstrap 95% confidence interval 0.19–0.68; naive plug-in mutual information reads 1.17 bits but the finite-sample bias floor alone is 0.75 bits), and this ceiling is front-end-independent — it moves by at most 0.16 bits across five embeddings including a nonlinear one. It is a sub-bit decision stamp, not the multi-bit code a naive estimate suggests. (2) A hardened negative. No re-placement of the cells beats plain reconstruction-driven k-means: an information-optimal (agglomerative information-bottleneck) quantizer overfits (held-out −0.014), curved-space quantization is a wash (+0.001), and a flow-aware "generating" symbolic partition is refuted (−0.021); a test-tuned oracle finds apparent headroom that a realizable train-selected rule cannot capture (information-bottleneck realizable −0.007, curvature −0.001). (3) A near-universal escape, and a map. The only lever that moves held-out accuracy is admitting more independent information — a partition-and-aggregate ladder — and we validate it on a new axis, frequency: a dyadic-band ladder lifts accuracy by +0.044 on average, is roughly two-thirds structural rather than byte-count, stacks on the channel ladder, and on a 105-dataset census escapes the single-token bound on 88% of the archive while the bound and its boundary both transfer to seven held-out datasets. The picture is a four-regime law — bound (one token saturates), ceiling (easy tasks already saturated: canonical bearing-fault identification sits at 0.998, no room), escape (multi-scale morphology: handwriting +0.221, valve-acoustic anomaly +0.204), and boundary (electroencephalography, where the ladder cannot help even with a healthy token). (4) A cheap meter, and silicon. Escape is not predictable from a label-free energy statistic, but a cheap, codebook-free joint-separability meter predicts it (Spearman ρ 0.75 archive-wide; leave-one-out precision 0.99), validated out-of-source on real bearing and acoustic data (3/3). Finally, the laddered token runs bit-exact (0/54 decision flips, float32) and deterministic at 1.66 ms per inference on a commodity Arm Cortex-M4F, with a calibrated int8 path agreeing 0.98 and an exact host port. Measured head-to-head on identical folds against a modern random-kernel classifier (ROCKET family), the laddered token is a cost–accuracy dial: mean accuracy climbs from 91% to 96% of ROCKET across one to four bytes — most of it in the first two — reaching 95.9% at ~500× fewer features, as a deterministic table lookup. It is a tunable, auditable operating point on the cost–accuracy plane, not a higher accuracy. Two of the techniques — shuffle-null bias subtraction and an oracle-versus-realizable control that separates a true lever from selection-bias headroom — are general disciplines applicable well beyond this token. We close with a deployment contract. The contribution is a precise, hardened, hardware-validated statement of exactly how much one byte decides, when more bytes help, and how to tell in advance — strong precisely because it refuses to overclaim. No new mechanism is introduced; every lever re-expresses filed or published methods.
