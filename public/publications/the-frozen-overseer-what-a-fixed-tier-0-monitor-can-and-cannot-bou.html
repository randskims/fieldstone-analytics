The Frozen Overseer: What a Fixed Tier-0 Monitor Can and Cannot Bound in a Self-Improving System — a pre-registered map, an impossibility, a dual-reference escape, and a reduction to practice on commodity silicon

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 2, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.23893944

Abstract

Discussion of recursive self-improvement (RSI) increasingly asks whether a fixed, auditable guardrail can remain a valid bound as the system it watches grows more capable — a guardrail small and deterministic enough to run on hardware the monitored system cannot modify, "outside the loop." We study the narrowest honest version of that question for a frozen Tier-0 monitor: a bit-exact, ≤256-cell nearest-centroid "decision-token" codebook (microjoules per decision), commissioned once and never retrained, placed around a self-improving learner. Across a pre-registered battery — a controllable self-improvement substrate, four real public datasets, an int8 edge port, an adaptive white-box adversary, a fifteen-probe red-team, a reduction to practice on two production microcontrollers, and a real-electrocardiogram validation of a proposed extension — we map six hypotheses with predictions fixed before measurement and negatives kept. The frozen input monitor is a reliable behavioral tripwire for overt departures (true-positive rate 1.00, bit-exact, runnable on separate silicon) but it is not a capability-containment bound: it is provably blind to in-envelope capability gain (area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve, AUROC, equal to 0.50, exact and representation-independent), and it false-alarms chronically on the system's own legitimate growth (false-alarm rate 0.84–0.98). The headline is an impossibility: for a fixed-reference monitor the flag-rate on a system's legitimate self-expansion is simultaneously the false alarm an operator wants to suppress and the "the envelope moved" evidence an operator wants to preserve — the same event counted once — so recalibrating the monitor to stop false-alarming is mathematically identical to going blind to the system's self-improvement. A dual anchor∥ghost construction (a frozen anchor keeping the commissioned reference, in parallel with a recalibrating ghost tracking the current envelope on a shared embedding) escapes the identifiability half of the impossibility as a continuous "drift-from-commissioned-self" meter (ghost false-alarm 0.05 and anchor shift-awareness 0.73 simultaneously; the meter tracks true movement at Spearman ρ = 0.97) — but only as a magnitude gauge, blind to on-manifold intent, and a stealthy adversary staying within the commissioned radius evades it. We harden every claim with a fifteen-probe adversarial battery, reduce the dual monitor to practice on a 32-bit Arm Cortex-M4F and a Cortex-M33 microcontroller with 0/358 token flips on both codebooks and the impossibility-and-escape reproduced bit-exactly on real bearing vibration in 8.5 kB float / 2.8 kB int8, and show that extending the ghost to higher-order (velocity, acceleration) recalibration does not improve oversight: acceleration adds nothing, and the velocity gain seen in smooth synthetic drift does not transfer to real step-like inter-patient drift. From this map we distill a short deployment contract — add a convex guard, triangulate world-drift with a canary-ghost, trigger recommissioning with a cheap label-free screen, and treat the meter as a magnitude that hands off to a human or semantic layer — which locates the construction within the scalable-oversight and human-oversight framing of emerging AI-safety practice and regulation. The contribution is a precise, hardened, hardware-validated statement of where a frozen Tier-0 interlock is and is not a valid bound — strong precisely because it refuses to overclaim.
