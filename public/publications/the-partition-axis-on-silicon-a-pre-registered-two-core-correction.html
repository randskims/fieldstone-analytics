The Partition Axis on Silicon: A Pre-Registered, Two-Core Correction of a Frozen Decision Token's Compute-Cost Map

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 12, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.24148007

Abstract

A companion study established, on a desktop compute proxy, that the feature front-end — the partition axis — of a frozen, deterministic, single-token decision encoder is a recalibration-free amplitude-drift invariance lever, and it ranked the five axes by a wall-clock-per-window cost measured on an x86 host (channel 1× · ordinal ≈2× · frequency ≈5× · recurrence ≈70× · topological ≈81×), from which it drew a deployment contract: ordinal when cost-constrained, microcontroller-plausible; topological when accuracy is paramount at ≈80× compute. That cost claim rests on a host proxy and was explicitly labelled as such. Here we port all five front-ends to bit-faithful C and measure them on real microcontroller silicon — a Cortex-M4F (Nordic nRF52840) and a Cortex-M33 (Nordic nRF54L15) — under a pre-registered protocol with frozen Go/No-Go bands and a stop-if-it-does-not-sing deposit gate. The headline result is a correction: on silicon the cost ordering swaps at the expensive end. Where the desktop proxy ranks the topological axis costliest (81× the channel axis) and recurrence second (70×), the M4F ranks recurrence costliest by far (123×) and topological far cheaper (34×); the Spearman rank-correlation between the on-silicon and desktop orderings is 0.90, with the single inversion exactly the topological↔recurrence pair. The mechanism is the memory hierarchy a desktop proxy hides: the recurrence front-end is bottlenecked by an O(L²) distance matrix (~97 KB, cache-hostile) plus a 12,403-element quantile sort, while the topological front-end uses a matrix-free minimum-spanning-tree construction with no stored matrix and no sort. The correction is not a one-board artifact — the M33 reproduces the identical ordering (Spearman vs. the M4F = 1.00; vs. the desktop proxy = 0.90), and every one of twelve pre-registered hardening tiers (per-axis current, true-idle floor, 8-channel scale-up, a hardware FFT, a 1.8 V point, a picojoule-per-cycle cross-check, a linker-map footprint, a window-length sweep, and a data-robustness pass) confirmed or sharpened it. Measured/analytic energy pins a deployment-ready tier boundary: an always-on tier (channel, ordinal) that duty-cycles below 1 mW at 1 Hz, a mid tier (frequency), and an edge tier (topological, recurrence) in which topological only marginally exceeds the always-on budget at 1 Hz (1.09 mW) while recurrence runs far over it (2.73 mW) and is disqualified on compute and memory (~145 KB) and, uniquely, on determinism. A fourth, previously unmeasured axis dimension emerges on-device: the ordinal front-end is the only one whose entire raw→token path is bit-exact across a float64 host and a float32 device (0 of 64 flips), where the recurrence (6%) and topological (25%) front-ends inherit their features' floating-point sensitivity — so ordinal dominates not only on compute, energy, and memory but on auditability. A pre-registered second thesis — that the rank-based relational token is cross-precision portable where the FFT-based channel token is not — is reported as an honest negative: the relational feature vectors are exactly precision-invariant, but on FPU silicon the channel token is equally token-stable, so the property confers no token-level advantage. The practical consequence is a corrected deployment map: ordinal is the cheap invariant on real silicon, confirmed on two cores and in energy, memory, and determinism; topological is a mid-tier front-end on compute and memory, not an ≈80× luxury; and recurrence, not topological, is the axis to avoid on a microcontroller. This is a characterization, on commodity hardware, of previously published methods that are the subject of filed U.S. patent applications; it discloses no new algorithmic subject matter. The per-deployment axis-selection and commissioning procedure is retained as trade secret.
