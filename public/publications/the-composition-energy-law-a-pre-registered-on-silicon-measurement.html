The Composition Energy Law: A Pre-Registered, On-Silicon Measurement of When Many Always-On Capabilities Share One Frozen Decision's Cost — and When They Do Not

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 21, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.24426651

Abstract

A frozen, deterministic, class-discriminant single-token decision encoder — a fixed projection and a nearest-of-256-centroid lookup that reduces a window of sensor observations to one 8-bit token — is not a single capability but a substrate. The same token already powers a whole family of always-on functions: the base decision, its nearest-centroid competence monitor, an on-device safety interlock, a streaming anomaly monitor, a multi-head token ladder, an amplitude-invariant ordinal front end, and a geometry-gated drift adaptation. A companion study measured the energy of one decision. This paper asks the deployment question that follows and that a careful evaluator asks first: what does running the whole stack cost on real silicon, and does each capability pay for itself? Under pre-registration — with Go/No-Go bands fixed before any measurement — and on the microcontrollers the method targets, we find a sharp and simple law. Composition is sub-additive: the full always-on Tier-0 stack costs 1.002 times a single decision on the host op decomposition, because every capability rides the one feature-extraction-plus-token computation and adds only a readout (a threshold on a distance the encoder already returns) or one bounded recompute; the ratio of K separate per-capability models to the shared-token composition is 3.7 times against a conservative feature-fed comparator and 29 times against a realistic raw-signal detector at K = 4, and that frugal multiple is linear in K and unbounded (measured 2.0, 4.0, 8.0, and 16.0 times at K = 2, 4, 8, 16, the composed stack staying flat near 6,080 ns while K separate models grow in proportion). The mechanism is not a definitional trick: measured call-counters show the composed K = 4 stack invokes the front end and the token encode exactly once each per decision against a naive stack's four and four — a measured reuse ratio of 3.87, approximately K. The crux is that the shared front end is 92% of the base decision, so the expensive part is paid once and each reused capability is nearly free. The marginal energy of a capability is governed by which of four classes it belongs to, and the classes carry four measured signatures: a stateless-free readout costs 0.037% of the base decision, a bounded-re-derivable recompute (the drift adaptation) 0.128%, an extra token on the ladder one more projection-and-search (about 7.7%), and a needs-persist keep-warm commit — the one genuinely expensive class — 860 microjoules per commit on the device's non-volatile memory, about 65 times a base decision, measured on silicon and dominated by erase latency. The token ladder, the one capability that materially moves the cost, has a measurable return-on-investment crossover: on the two modalities with accuracy headroom on a matching partition axis it pays fast (ECG +7.3 points to a crossover depth D* = 2; human-activity recognition +1.9 points to D* = 3) and saturates fast (every doubling past the knee buys less than 0.5 points), while on a saturated signal (bearing fault, already 0.996) or a mismatched axis it is dead weight — the “No Winning Ticket” of energy, with a sharp knee. The composed sizing law follows: the decision-only minimum storage capacitor rises only to 1.28 times the single-token capacitor at a useful ladder depth, and the full boot-to-first-decision capacitor to 1.0007 times, because the platform system-wake dominates the per-power-up budget by about 313 times and the capabilities are 0.07% of the deployment capacitor. Everything is reproduced on two cores — a Nordic nRF54L15 (Cortex-M33) metered with a Power Profiler Kit II and an nRF52840 (Cortex-M4F) — with the composed decision bit-exact across both (0 of 256 token flips, 0 of 54 ladder flips on each core, 0 cross-core flips), so the law is not an artifact of one compiler or one core. On silicon the single dominant energy lever is confirmed to be the shared front end: one front-end pass measures 139 microjoules, so the ladder's separate front end for depth 4 is roughly 1.1 millijoules — which dwarfs the ladder decision itself (57 microjoules) and is exactly what a shared-spectrum ladder removes. We report the honest boundaries with equal prominence: composition is sub-additive only for capabilities that read out the shared token; a capability with its own transform (an ordinal, topological, or recurrence front end) is additive, costing from 2.3 to 95 times the base decision, so the law's boundary is the front-end-sharing boundary. A needs-persist commit is expensive and breaks the band if committed too often on erase-based memory. An extra ladder token past the knee is dead weight. And the frequently-cited int8 energy win does not transfer to a core with a hardware floating-point unit — on the Cortex-M33 the int8 base decision is 8% worse, not cheaper — so that lever is specific to floating-point-less microcontrollers, though sub-additivity itself is arithmetic-independent. The law is not specific to the Fourier transform: the frugal multiple is approximately K for three different shared transforms, and holds on a fourth sensor modality (surface electromyography). The whole result is a characterization of previously published, separately patent-pending matter; it introduces no new claimed mechanism, and the per-deployment selection procedure is retained as trade secret.
