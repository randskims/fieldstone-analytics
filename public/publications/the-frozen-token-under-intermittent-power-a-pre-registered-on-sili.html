The Frozen Token Under Intermittent Power: A Pre-Registered, On-Silicon Demonstration that a Frozen Single-Token Decision Survives Repeated Power Failure Bit-Exact by Progress-Free Atomic Re-Execution

Randolph James Ferlic & Kimberly Kate Ferlic · Preprint · Nov 19, 2026 · Zenodo

Download PDFView on Zenododoi.org/zenodo.24374591

Abstract

A batteryless sensor draws its energy from light, radio, heat, or motion, buffers it in a small capacitor, and is therefore powered on and off repeatedly and unpredictably — often many times per second. Every power failure erases volatile memory, so any computation longer than a single charge must be preserved across the failure or it never completes. The intermittent-computing literature preserves it: it checkpoints activations, or persists a loop index and resumes from the point of failure, or commits idempotent tasks — machinery whose premise is that the computation is long relative to a charge. This paper measures what happens when the computation is not long. The method under test is a frozen, deterministic, class-discriminant single-token decision encoder — a fixed projection and a nearest-of-≤256-centroid lookup that reduces a window of sensor observations to one 8-bit token — whose entire decision is a bounded (~1.57 ms) pure function of a single input window and a fixed model, carrying no state between windows. We show, under two pre-registered campaigns with frozen Go/No-Go bands, that such a decision survives power failure not by any progress-preservation machinery but by progress-free atomic re-execution: on power-up the device restores no intermediate state and tracks no progress index, and simply re-executes the entire fixed computation afresh over a persisted copy of the input window, re-deriving a decision that is bit-exact identical across an arbitrary number of power interruptions, because it keeps no progress to lose. The persisted state is the input window alone; the activation checkpoint is zero bytes, against the 33 KB of genuine computed activations a comparison int8 convolutional network must checkpoint and restore on every power cycle. We measured the decision re-deriving bit-exact across 15,136 cold-start decision comparisons (from independent reboot processes) with zero disagreements (Rule-of-Three 95 % upper bound < 2.0×10⁻⁴), across 24 of 24 mid-computation hardware-reset injections, and — the gold standard — across real external removals of the supply on a Nordic nRF54L15 (Cortex-M33) instrumented with a Power Profiler Kit II, in which the device's volatile memory was wholly lost and it rebooted from an unpowered state, the same token re-deriving bit-exact every time; the result holds identically at 1.8 V and on a second core (nRF52840, Cortex-M4F). The one genuine piece of state — the input window — is persisted by a power-failure-atomic protocol (double buffer + cyclic-redundancy check + commit indicator), which we hammered with 4,000 torn-write injections (3,188 interrupted mid-commit) and observed zero corrupt reads. The resume overhead is not an activation checkpoint but the rebuilding of volatile working tables, which we measured (≈2.1 ms / 16.4 µJ for a transform front-end with a RAM-cached model) and reduced to approximately zero with a stateless ordinal front-end and a flash-resident model. We settle the standard objection — that re-executing an interrupted computation wastes work — with a measured energy law: the re-execution waste scales with the ratio of the decision time to the mean time between failures and therefore vanishes for a bounded decision (8.1 % at a 10 ms mean on-time, 0.08 % at 1 s), so progress-free re-execution is the energy-optimal strategy precisely in the regime this token occupies, where a 756 ms int8 network is instead checkpoint-mandatory. From the measured energies we derive the forward-progress threshold (the token completes from a ~4–10 µF capacitor where the int8 network needs ~2 mF, a 206× capacitor-size advantage) and the non-volatile-memory endurance (a byte-writable FRAM/RRAM with wear-leveled double-buffering lasts tens of thousands of years at one decision per second, where a block-erase flash is exhausted in days). We establish that the survival property is architectural, not an artifact of one encoder or dataset, by re-deriving the decision bit-exact across 3,872 cold boots spanning six distinct decision computations and four signal modalities (transform, ordinal, recurrence, topological, and a four-head product-of-posteriors token ladder). We disclose the invention's boundaries honestly and do not claim them surpassed: a forward-progress threshold shared by every classifier that does not sub-divide and checkpoint; an uncommitted-transient-input acquisition limit shared by every sensor; the one adaptation-carrying sibling (a geometry-gated drift compensator) whose ≤152-byte state either persists more cheaply than the window or, if lost, degrades gracefully to a defined commissioning-anchor decision and re-converges geometrically over a median 41 windows with no hard-failure mode; and a platform system-wake (≈833 ms full-OS boot, trimmed to ≈532 ms) that dominates the per-power-up time and is a separable platform cost, not a token property. The progress-free re-execution mechanism and the window-persistence protocol are the subject of a U.S. provisional patent application the authors filed before this deposit; the base encoder, its ladder, nearest-centroid monitor, and drift adapter are previously published and separately patent-pending; the per-deployment harvesting-aware selection procedure is retained as trade secret.
